Overview

The Boeing 737 MAX crisis arose from a series of engineering, organizational, and regulatory decisions that culminated in two fatal crashes: Lion Air Flight 610 in October 2018 and Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 in March 2019. Together, these crashes claimed 346 lives.

Design & Organizational Decisions

MCAS relied on a single sensor, hidden from flight crews while pressure to avoid retraining shaped every decision.

  • Single point of failure violated redundancy principles
  • Organizational pressure rewarded speed over transparency
  • Delegated certification limited FAA ability to intervene

Ethical Issues & Professional Duty

ASME Canon 1 was breached when public safety was subordinated to schedule and cost.

  • Engineers faced pressure to downplay risk signals
  • Pilots received incomplete training and documentation
  • Regulators depended on manufacturer data without independent review

Lessons Learned

Preventable steps existed at every stage—from initial design to the decision not to ground the fleet after the first crash.

  • Enforce redundant sensors and envelope limits
  • Honest pilot training and transparent manuals
  • Precautionary grounding once systemic risk is evident

Personal Reflections

Studying the Boeing 737 MAX crisis fundamentally reshaped my understanding of engineering responsibility. I learned that technical competence alone is insufficient; ethical judgment, institutional courage, and systems thinking are equally essential. Engineers do not operate in isolation, and ethical failures often arise not from malice, but from incremental compromises made under pressure.

This case reinforced the importance of asking uncomfortable questions, especially when incentives push toward speed and convenience. It demonstrated that compliance with minimum regulatory standards does not guarantee ethical integrity, and that silence in the face of unsafe decisions can be as consequential as active wrongdoing.

Key Takeaway: Engineers must view themselves as stewards of public trust. When systems are safety-critical, ethical responsibility does not end at one's job description. Upholding safety may require resisting organizational pressure, escalating concerns beyond formal channels, or refusing to approve flawed designs, even at personal or professional cost.
